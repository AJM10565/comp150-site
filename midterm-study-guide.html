Midterm Study Guide

Course COMP 150-003 · Introduction to Computing Term Fall 2026 Covers Assignment 1 · Quiz 1 · Assignments 2–4 · Weeks 3, 5 & 6 labs

This guide covers everything we have assessed so far. If you can do everything on this list without looking anything up, you are in good shape. Everything here is fair game again on the (cumulative, open cheat-sheet) final — studying now is studying twice.

How to use this guide: don't just read it. For every ✅ item, actually do it — on paper or in a fresh Colab cell — and check yourself against the posted solutions and the Quiz 1 key.

1 · Digital Information (Assignment 1, Part A)

Core ideas

  • Interpreters vs. compilers: how each translates code, trade-offs (speed of execution vs. speed of development, portability), and an example language of each.
  • How text becomes binary: characters → code points (ASCII/UTF-8) → bits.
  • How bits travel: radio (modulated waves), electrical (voltage levels), optical (light pulses).
✅ Skills to practice
  • Encode a short string in ASCII: give decimal and binary codes, and compute its size in bytes (don't forget spaces and punctuation!).
  • Know why ASCII and UTF-8 (Plane 0) sizes can match for plain English text.
  • Convert decimal numbers to binary, octal, and hexadecimal — showing your work.

2 · Python Names, Types & Expressions (Assignment 2 · Quiz 1)

Core ideas

  • Valid names (variables and functions): letters, digits, underscores; can't start with a digit; no hyphens (- is minus!), no #/@; keywords are off-limits (while, for, def, try, class…). _temp and updateProfile are valid.
  • Boolean expressions: comparisons (== vs. =!), and/or/not, string comparison is alphabetical, mixing types (10 <= "10") is an error.
  • Arithmetic: precedence ** before * / // % before + -; ties left-to-right; // floor division (watch negatives: -7 // 2 == -4), % remainder, / always gives a float.
✅ Skills to practice
  • For each name in A2 Problems 1–2: valid or invalid, and why.
  • Evaluate boolean expressions by hand and explain each step (Quiz 1 Q2).
  • Compute 19 // 4 and 19 % 4 style pairs and explain via quotient × divisor + remainder.
  • Evaluate mixed expressions like 10 - 2 * 3 + 2 ** 2 step-by-step (Quiz 1 Q4).
⚠️ Common traps (straight from the Quiz 1 key)
  • A hyphen is never part of a name — Python reads it as subtraction.
  • ** before *//, which come before +/-; ties go left to right.
  • not applies before and, which applies before or.
  • range(a, b) includes a but excludes b.

3 · Loops, Lists & Functions (Assignments 2–3 · Quiz 1 Q5)

Core ideas

  • One task, many ways (A2 Problem 5): for over range, for over a list, index loop with range(len(...)), while loop, list comprehension, and wrapping it in a function.
  • Accumulator patterns: running totals, building lists with .append(), counting with conditions.
  • Tracing loops by hand — the trace table. One row per iteration, tracking each variable. It appeared on Quiz 1 (Q5), A3 Problem 7, and A4 Problem 4. It will appear again.
✅ Skills to practice
  • Predict what a short loop prints (e.g., total = 0; for i in range(1, 4): total += i * i → 14) and show the trace table.
  • Write squares_up_to(n) all six ways from A2 Problem 5.
  • The streak pattern (A3 Problems 6–7): two counters, current_streak and best_streak; reset on a miss, max(...) to keep the best. Trace it before you code it.
  • The classic column jobs from A3: consecutive differences (velocities), month-by-month accumulation (compound balance), filter-then-average (reaction times), count + max in one pass (blood pressure), moving average over a window, drop-lowest-then-average with sorted(...) and slicing.

4 · Debugging, Comments & Algorithms (Assignment 2)

Core ideas

  • Finding logic bugs in code that runs but gives wrong answers (A2 Problem 6).
  • Good comments explain why, not what (# keep this one — it passed the cutoff beats # add n to the list).
  • What makes a list of steps an algorithm: precision, order, a clear stopping point (wolf/goat/cabbage).
✅ Skills to practice
  • Given a short buggy function, find the bug by tracing, not by staring.
  • Add a one-line "what it does" comment plus why-comments to an uncommented function.

5 · Dictionaries (Assignment 4)

Core ideas

  • Records come as lists of items or lists of dictionaries; the answer is always two steps: organize into a dictionary, then ask it a question.
  • The counting pattern: counts[key] = counts.get(key, 0) + 1.
  • Safe lookups with .get(...) and in; missing keys as a case to handle (missing item = 0 on the shelf; play not in library = skip).
  • Finding the top entry by hand (loop + comparison), including tie-breaking alphabetically — not max(key=...).
  • Building a result dictionary that includes every team/species, even those with 0.
✅ Skills to practice
  • Re-do the five A4 patterns from scratch: tally + rare items (bird survey), threshold comparison across two dicts (restock), tally + argmax with ties (clinic), points table from game records (league table), lookup-and-accumulate (playlist minutes).

6 · Testing & TDD (Assignments 3–4 · Week 6)

Core ideas

  • Reading assert-style tests and understanding what they claim (A3: provided tests).
  • TDD cycle: Plan (comments first) → Red (write tests, watch them fail) → Green (write code until they pass) → Refactor (stay green).
  • Choosing tests: normal (typical), special (ties, repeats, missing entries), edge (empty list, one record, exactly at a limit) — with a why-comment on every test.
✅ Skills to practice
  • Given a function description, write 4–8 tests covering normal/special/edge before writing the function.
  • Explain why a failing test at the Red step is a good sign.

7 · Your Paper (Current Events in AI paper)

You wrote it — now own it. Review your own paper closely enough that you could discuss it without having it in front of you:

  • What stance you took, and the main arguments you made for it.
  • The key evidence and sources you cited, and why you chose them.
  • What the strongest counterargument to your position is, and how you'd answer it.

The AI policy applies to papers just like code: you must be able to explain — and stand behind — every sentence you submitted.

How to study (in priority order)

  1. Redo, don't reread. Reopen A2–A4 in fresh Colab cells and re-solve 2–3 problems from each without looking at your old answers.
  2. Trace by hand. Pick any loop from A3/A4 and build the trace table on paper. This is the single most-tested skill so far.
  3. Drill the traps. The "Common traps" box in Section 2 is a quiz-question factory.
  4. Check against the keys. Compare your reasoning, not just your answers.
  5. Reasoning is your insurance. Per course policy, a bare answer without the why gambles for full credit or zero. Practice explaining out loud.
