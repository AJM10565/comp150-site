Final Project: Solve a Real Problem with Real Data

Team 2–3 students Worth 400 pts + up to 500 extra credit Replaces the final exam Check-ins Tue Nov 3 · Nov 10 · Nov 17 Final + presentations Tue Dec 1 (last class)

The short version: pick a problem real people have. Find real data about it. Use that data to understand the problem. Then build something that helps (an app, dashboard, tool, or bot) or write a report that recommends solutions backed by your data. Present it to the class on Dec 1.

This project replaces the final exam. It is less about writing clever code and more about what this course is really for: using a computer, and the tools now available to you (including your AI harness), to understand and solve a real problem. Good design, honest use of data, and clear communication earn the points. Ambitious engineering earns the extra credit.

How it works: three check-ins, then the finish

Each check-in is a short write-up submitted on Sakai by 11:00 PM on the date listed (one submission per team), plus a 5-minute conversation with me in class that night. The check-ins are there to catch problems early. I will tell you if your problem is too big, your data won’t work, or your design is missing something. No one should get to December with a project that can’t be finished.

Check-in 1: Problem & team (25 pts, Nov 3)

1–2 pages:

  • Team: names, and how you’ll communicate and share files.
  • The problem: who has it, and why it matters to them. Be specific: “CTA riders on the 22 Clark bus can’t tell whether to wait or walk” beats “public transit is bad.”
  • The data: at least one real data source you’ve actually opened (link it, and say what’s in it: rows, columns, date range). A list of 20 starter problems with data is below.
  • App or report? Your first guess at which path fits (see Two paths below). You can change it later.

I’ll approve the topic, or ask you to adjust it, within a few days.

Check-in 2: Data in hand (50 pts, Nov 10)

  • The data downloaded or connected, and opened in a Colab notebook (or a spreadsheet, if that’s honestly the right tool).
  • What’s in it: size, columns, date range, and what one row means.
  • What’s wrong with it: missing values, odd entries, gaps. Every real dataset has some.
  • Three questions your data can answer, each with a first chart or table that starts to answer it.
  • One surprise: something in the data you didn’t expect.

Check-in 3: Design (50 pts, Nov 17)

App path:

  • Who the user is, and the one main thing they come to do.
  • A sketch or mockup of each screen (paper photos are fine; Figma or similar is great).
  • How the data gets to the user: what’s computed ahead of time vs. live.
  • What you’ll build it with (Colab + widgets, Streamlit, Google Sheets + Apps Script, Glide, a website, …), and why.

Report path:

  • An outline of the report, with each section’s claim in one sentence.
  • The charts you plan to include (drafts are fine).
  • Your candidate solutions, and what in the data would tell you which one is best.

Both paths: a task split (who owns what) and a schedule to Dec 1.

Final deliverable (175 pts, Dec 1)

  • App path: a working app I can use, plus a short README: what it does, who it’s for, how to run it, where the data comes from.
  • Report path: a 6–12 page report (charts count toward length) with an executive summary, the problem, your data and method, findings, recommended solutions, limits, and sources.
  • Both: the notebook(s) or code that turn the raw data into your results, with comments explaining why. Plus an AI usage disclosure, the same as on every other assignment.

Presentation (75 pts, Dec 1)

8–10 minutes per team, plus questions. Everyone on the team speaks. Show the problem, the data, and what you built or found. For an app, demo it live. Close with what you’d do next.

Individual reflection + peer review (25 pts, Dec 4)

Each student submits this separately:

  • Your contributions: what you did, specifically.
  • One thing you learned.
  • A short, confidential rating of each teammate’s contribution.

Two paths

Build an app when the problem is one that a tool could fix: people lack information at the moment they need it, or have to do something tedious by hand. Example: a page that tells you, for your block, how many days until street sweeping, and texts you the night before.

Write a report when the fix is a decision, a policy, or a change in behavior. Example: using crash data to recommend which five Chicago intersections most need a protected left turn, and why.

Either path can earn full credit. Pick the one that fits your problem.

Grading

How team grades work

Check-ins 1–3, the final deliverable, and the presentation earn a team score: everyone on the team gets the same points. The individual reflection is scored per student. If peer reviews and your git/Drive history clearly show a teammate didn’t contribute, I will adjust that student’s team score down. I will talk to the team first. If your team is having trouble, tell me early. I can only help with problems I know about.

20 starter problems (with real data)

You may pick one of these, or bring your own as long as you can show me real data for it at Check-in 1. Many of the Chicago datasets live on the Chicago Data Portal, and most can be downloaded as CSV with one click.

# Problem Who has it Data
1 Is my bus actually coming? Which CTA routes are least reliable, and when? CTA riders CTA ridership, CTA Bus Tracker API
2 Will there be a Divvy bike? Stations that run empty or full at rush hour Bike commuters Divvy trip data, Divvy live station feed (GBFS)
3 Dangerous intersections: where crashes cluster, and what would help Pedestrians, cyclists, drivers Chicago traffic crashes
4 Potholes and broken streetlights: how fast does the city fix them, and is it fair across neighborhoods? Residents Chicago 311 service requests
5 Is this restaurant safe? Food inspection results, made easy to check Diners Chicago food inspections
6 Can I swim today? Lake Michigan beach water quality Beachgoers, families Chicago beach E. coli lab results, Beach water quality, automated sensors
7 Lead in drinking water: which homes still have lead service lines? Renters, parents Chicago lead service line inventory, EPA SDWIS
8 Bad air days: when and where air quality is unhealthy People with asthma, runners EPA AirNow API, EPA AirData
9 Food deserts: neighborhoods far from a grocery store Families without cars USDA Food Access Research Atlas
10 Can I afford to live here? Rent vs. income by neighborhood Students, young workers Census ACS (data.census.gov), HUD Fair Market Rents
11 Evictions: where they happen and what predicts them Renters, advocates Eviction Lab
12 Is this college worth it? Cost, debt, and earnings by school and major High-school seniors, transfer students College Scorecard
13 What jobs pay, and what they need: wages and skills by occupation Students choosing majors BLS Occupational Employment & Wages, O*NET
14 Will my flight be late? Delays by airline, airport, and time of day Travelers BTS on-time performance
15 Energy-wasting buildings: which large buildings use the most energy? Tenants, city planners Chicago Energy Benchmarking
16 Health gaps by neighborhood: asthma, diabetes, no checkups Public-health workers CDC PLACES
17 Scams: which frauds are rising, and who they target Older adults, students FTC Consumer Sentinel data
18 Extreme heat: who is most at risk during heat waves? Seniors, outdoor workers NOAA climate data, Heat.gov (NIHHIS)
19 Drug prices: which prescriptions cost the most, and are there cheaper equivalents? Patients CMS Medicare Part D spending
20 Safe routes to school / campus: crime and lighting along walking routes Students Chicago crimes (2001–present), 311 streetlight outages

Bringing your own topic? Great! The test is simple: by Check-in 1, link a real dataset you have opened, and tell me who has the problem.

Rules of the road

  • AI tools are welcome, including your pi harness. Use them to build more than you could alone. You must disclose how you used them, and every team member must be able to explain every part of what you submit. I will ask in the presentation Q&A.
  • Cite your data. Every chart says where its numbers came from.
  • No personal data about real people beyond what’s already public and aggregated. If your idea needs it, talk to me first.
  • Late check-ins: the same flexible policy as the rest of the course. But a late check-in means late feedback, which hurts you. The Dec 1 presentation can’t move.
