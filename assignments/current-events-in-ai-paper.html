COMP 150 Fall 2026 — Current Events in AI Paper

Course: COMP 150-003, Fall 2026 Worth: 50 points (this is your “midterm”) Official due: Friday, October 2, 11:00 PM (counts toward your mid-term grade) Grace deadline: Wednesday, October 14, 11:00 PM (no penalty, but the score counts toward your final grade instead of your mid-term grade) Length: 1000–2000 words (excluding citations) Submission: One PDF via Sakai — LastName_FirstName_COMP150_Paper.pdf

Introduction

Artificial intelligence is no longer a topic that waits patiently in textbooks. The events shaping it — product launches, lawsuits, breakthroughs, failures, regulations — are happening right now, and their consequences will land squarely on your generation. This assignment asks you to engage with one of the two defining AI controversies of 2026, anchor your analysis in a real, dated current event, and take a clear, well-argued ethical stance.

Objective

Choose one of the two topics below. Articulate a coherent, defensible position on the question it poses. You must:

  1. Anchor your paper in a current event — a specific, dated news event from 2026 (a product release, incident, lawsuit, policy action, research result, or public controversy). This event is your hook and your evidence base, not a decoration.
  2. Develop a clear thesis and defend it throughout with logical reasoning and cited evidence.
  3. Acknowledge the strongest counterarguments to your position and respond to them honestly.

This is a stance essay, not a summary. “Both sides have good points” is not a thesis.

Topic A: AI Agents and Digital Agency

Who is responsible when your AI acts for you?

In this course you are building and operating your own AI harness — an agent that reads, writes, and executes on your behalf. Millions of people now delegate real work to AI agents: sending messages, making purchases, writing code, filing paperwork. This delegation raises the central question: when an AI agent acts on a person’s behalf, who owns the outcome — and who should be allowed to delegate what?

Address the following (weights are a guide):

  • Introduction & Stance (10–15%) — State your position on the ethics of delegating action (not just advice) to AI agents. Preview your key arguments.
  • Your Current Event (15–20%) — Present your anchor event: a specific 2026 incident, launch, or controversy involving AI agents acting autonomously. Explain what happened and why it matters.
  • Responsibility & Accountability (15–20%) — When an agent causes harm — a wrong purchase, a leaked secret, a damaging message, broken code in production — who is accountable: the user, the developer, the model provider, or the agent’s own logs? Defend a principle, not just a case-by-case shrug.
  • Autonomy vs. Oversight (15–20%) — How much should agents be allowed to do without human confirmation? Where should the “human in the loop” be mandatory? Consider both convenience and catastrophe.
  • Agency Inequality (10–15%) — If capable agents multiply what one person can do, what happens to people without access, skills, or money to run them? Is “digital agency” becoming a new axis of inequality?
  • Balancing Progress & Responsibility (10–15%) — Propose specific practices, norms, or policies consistent with your stance. What should users, companies, or regulators actually do?
  • Concluding Thoughts (10–15%) — Reiterate your stance with conviction. What principles should govern the age of delegated intelligence?

Topic B: The Race to Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)

Should humanity be racing to build minds smarter than our own?

Frontier labs now openly state that their goal is superintelligence — AI that exceeds human capability across essentially every domain. Enormous capital, compute, and talent are being poured into getting there first, while critics range from “this is the most important project in history” to “this is the most dangerous.” The question: is the current race to ASI ethically defensible — and if not, what should change?

Address the following (weights are a guide):

  • Introduction & Stance (10–15%) — State your position on the race to superintelligence: full speed ahead, proceed with specific guardrails, slow down/pause, or something else. Preview your key arguments.
  • Your Current Event (15–20%) — Present your anchor event: a specific 2026 announcement, capability result, safety incident, investment, or policy action in the race toward ASI. Explain what happened and why it matters.
  • Promise vs. Peril (15–20%) — Weigh the strongest claimed benefits (science, medicine, abundance) against the strongest claimed risks (loss of control, misuse, concentration of power). Engage the best version of the side you disagree with.
  • Who Decides? (15–20%) — A handful of companies and countries are making a species-level decision. Is that legitimate? What role should governments, international bodies, and the public play? Consider the competition problem: if one actor slows down, others may not.
  • Alignment & Control (10–15%) — Can systems smarter than us be made to reliably pursue human values? Assess the state of the art honestly — what do we actually know how to do, and what are we merely hoping?
  • Balancing Progress & Responsibility (10–15%) — Propose specific policies, agreements, or practices consistent with your stance. What should labs, governments, or citizens actually do?
  • Concluding Thoughts (10–15%) — Reiterate your stance with conviction. What principles should govern the pursuit of superintelligence?

Research Requirements

Include at least 5 credible sources, with at least:

  • 1 news article from 2026 documenting your anchor event (this one is mandatory and load-bearing)
  • 2 substantive sources — peer-reviewed papers, technical reports, or in-depth industry/policy analyses
  • 2 additional credible sources of your choice

Given how fast this field moves, prefer sources from the last 2 years except where citing foundational work.

Research starting points

  • Reporting: MIT Technology Review, Wired, The Verge, Ars Technica, Reuters/AP technology desks
  • Analysis & policy: Stanford HAI, AI Now Institute, Brookings, RAND, Ada Lovelace Institute
  • Primary sources: frontier-lab announcements, system cards, and safety frameworks; government AI policy documents (EU AI Act, US executive actions, state legislation)
  • Topic A: search “AI agent incident,” “agentic AI liability,” “computer-use agents,” “AI agent security”
  • Topic B: search “superintelligence race,” “AI scaling,” “frontier AI safety,” “AI pause debate,” “compute governance”

Tips for Success

  • Pick your anchor event first — the right event makes the paper write itself.
  • A strong stance you partially walk back beats a mushy stance you fully defend.
  • Be specific with facts: names, dates, numbers, quotes.
  • Steelman the other side; a paper that only fights strawmen earns strawman points.
